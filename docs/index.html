How do you test a random number?

You can't — a single number is neither random nor not. What you can test is a source: take a long stream of its output, compute statistics whose distributions are known for true randomness, and see whether the stream behaves the way genuine coin flips would. This site walks through the statistical theory behind those tests and reviews the major test suites that grew out of it, from Marsaglia's Diehard battery to TestU01 and PractRand, plus the specialised tests applied to cryptographic and non-cryptographic hash functions.

The one-sentence summary of the whole field: a statistical test can prove a generator is bad, but no collection of tests can prove one is good — passing means only that the specific defects tested for were not found. For cryptography this caveat is load-bearing: NIST itself states that statistical testing "cannot serve as a substitute for cryptanalysis" [NIST SP 800-22].

Why this matters

Pseudorandom number generators sit under Monte-Carlo simulation, statistical sampling, games, and every key ever generated. A defective generator silently corrupts results: the most famous example, IBM's RANDU, shipped in the 1960s and confined every consecutive triple of its outputs to 15 planes in the unit cube — invisible in casual use, catastrophic for any 3-dimensional simulation, and provable in two lines of modular arithmetic [Marsaglia 1968]. You can rotate RANDU's point cloud yourself on the classical-tests page and watch the planes snap into view.

Hash functions face the same question from a different angle: a good hash should behave like a random function of its input. The hash-function page covers the avalanche and bit-independence criteria, SMHasher, collision statistics, and the sharp lesson of MurmurHash — a hash that passed every statistical test yet fell to seed-independent collision attacks [Aumasson & Bernstein 2012].

Which test sees which defect

The organising fact of the field: tests and defects pair up. A battery is a portfolio of narrow instruments, and the interesting cells are the blind ones — whole rows of respectable tests that a specific defect walks straight past. Hover any cell for the one-line reason; every claim links to the page that defends it.

About this site

Every figure on this site is computed live in your browser by small, dependency-free JavaScript modules — the same statistics the text describes, run on generators seeded deterministically so you see what the text claims you'll see. The source is laid out and documented in the repository README, with cross-references between each page and the modules behind its figures. This site is a sibling of the probsim project (source) and follows its conventions: the published site lives in docs/, sources and documentation cross-reference each other, open-access references can be fetched locally by script, and structural tests enforce the sync. Where probsim's paper explains the chi-squared, Student-t and Gumbel distributions, this site shows the tests those distributions power.
