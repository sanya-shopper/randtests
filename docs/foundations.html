Foundations: testing a hypothesis of randomness

Every randomness test, from the simplest bit-count to the largest battery in BigCrush, is the same move performed with different statistics: assume the stream is truly random, compute something whose distribution that assumption pins down, and ask how surprising the observed value is.

The framework

The null hypothesis H₀ says the sequence was produced by an ideal source: independent, uniformly distributed bits (or uniform values on [0,1)). A test picks a statistic T — the number of ones, the length of the longest run, the rank of a bit matrix — whose distribution under H₀ is known exactly or asymptotically. From the observed value it computes a p-value: the probability, under H₀, that the statistic would be at least as extreme as what was seen. Small p-value → the observation is surprising under randomness → reject H₀ [NIST SP 800-22].

Two kinds of error bound the enterprise. A Type I error rejects a truly random source; its probability is the significance level α, which the tester chooses — NIST suggests values around 0.01 for cryptographic work, meaning one in a hundred perfectly good sequences gets flagged by design. A Type II error accepts a defective source, and its probability β cannot be fixed in advance: "non-random" is not one alternative but infinitely many, and every defect has its own β. A test battery is an attempt to make β small against the defects that occur in practice [NIST SP 800-22].

Passing proves nothing. A test can only fail to reject. The binary digits of π pass every battery on this site; so does AES output under a known key. Both are completely deterministic. Statistical testing screens for the defects it looks for — no more. The converse direction, though, is solid: a reproducible failure is a proof of non-randomness.

One more consequence of the design: with m tests each run at level α, a perfect generator still fails about m·α of them. A battery of 100 tests at α = 0.01 is expected to produce one failure on flawless input. Suites handle this multiple-testing problem in different ways — NIST with pass-proportion intervals, Dieharder and TestU01 with second-level p-values, PractRand and SMHasher3 by widening thresholds with the test count — but the reader of any test report must handle it too: one marginal p-value is noise, a repeatable extreme one is a verdict.

The workhorse: Pearson's chi-square

Most randomness tests reduce to counting. Split the possible outcomes into k categories with null probabilities p1…pk, observe counts Oi in n trials against expectations Ei = n·pi, and form

χ² = Σi (Oi − Ei)² / Ei

Under H₀ this converges to the chi-square distribution with k − 1 degrees of freedom (fewer if parameters were estimated from the data), so a p-value comes from the upper tail: in NIST's notation, p = igamc(ν/2, χ²/2), the regularized upper incomplete gamma function. The approximation needs the expected counts to be reasonably large — the classical rule of thumb is every Ei ≥ 5, merging bins if necessary [Knuth 1997]. The statistic dates to Pearson's 1900 paper, the founding document of goodness-of-fit testing [Pearson 1900]; the degrees-of-freedom correction for estimated parameters came from Fisher in the 1920s.

Chi-square is everywhere on this site: the frequency, serial, gap, poker and coupon-collector tests are chi-square tests over different categoriza­tions (classical tests); most of the NIST battery funnels into igamc (NIST STS); ENT reports a raw chi-square on byte counts (ENT); SMHasher's bucket distribution tests are chi-square in disguise (hash functions).

Kolmogorov–Smirnov: goodness of fit without bins

When the statistic under test is continuous, binning throws information away. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test compares the empirical distribution function Fn of n observations directly against the hypothesized CDF F:

Dn = supx |Fn(x) − F(x)|

Under H₀, √n·Dn has a known limiting distribution independent of F (as long as F is continuous), which is what makes the test distribution-free [Kolmogorov 1933] [Smirnov 1948]. Knuth treats KS and chi-square as the two pillars: chi-square for categorical data, KS for continuous statistics — and, crucially, KS applied on top of other tests [Knuth 1997].

Two-level testing: testing the p-values

If T has a continuous distribution under H₀, then its p-value is itself uniformly distributed on [0,1) — this is the probability integral transform, and it is the single most useful fact in the field. Run the same test on 1000 disjoint stretches of output and you get 1000 numbers that, for a good generator, must look like uniform draws. So test them: apply KS or chi-square to the batch of p-values. This "two-level" scheme catches defects too small to trip any single run — a slight bias shifts every p-value a little, and the shift accumulates across the batch. It also catches generators that are too good: statistics hugging their expectations produce p-values bunched near ½, which a uniformity test flags just as loudly [Knuth 1997].

The batteries institutionalise this. NIST requires both a pass-proportion check and a chi-square uniformity test on the p-values of each of its tests (details on the NIST page); Dieharder converts every Diehard test into a KS test over many repeated p-values (Dieharder); TestU01 reports p-values of p-values throughout [L'Ecuyer & Simard 2007]. One caveat travels with the technique: second-level tests amplify not only real defects but also any error in the first-level null distribution. If the first-level p-values are only asymptotically valid, a large enough second level will eventually "detect" the approximation rather than the generator [L'Ecuyer & Simard 2007].
