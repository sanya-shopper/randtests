Beyond the big five

A short tour of the suites and standards that round out the landscape: two independent batteries with their own good ideas, the parallel-streams problem, and the two regulatory frameworks that test entropy sources rather than generators.

gjrand

A C library and battery ("mcp") by the pseudonymous "Geronimo Jones," notable for tests built on unusual statistics — its mod-3 test inspired PractRand's mod3n, a lineage PractRand's changelog credits explicitly [Doty-Humphrey]. gjrand has a reputation among generator authors for occasionally catching defects the mainstream suites miss, precisely because its tests are not aligned to the power-of-two structure most generators and most tests share. Documentation is sparse and maintenance quiet; it is best treated as a supplementary opinion [gjrand].

RaBiGeTe

Cristiano P.M.'s "Random Bit Generators Tester" — a Windows-based, multithreaded suite with a large test collection and a GUI that plots p-value distributions graphically. It found a niche following in the sci.crypt era and is effectively dormant, but its emphasis on looking at the distribution of p-values rather than thresholding them is pedagogically sound — it is the visual version of two-level testing [RaBiGeTe].

SPRNG: the parallel-streams problem

Serial suites answer whether one stream is defective. Parallel computing raises a different question: are ten thousand streams, drawn from the same generator family with different seeds or parameters, defective together? Inter-stream correlation can ruin a simulation whose every individual stream is impeccable. The SPRNG library (Mascagni & Srinivasan) ships tests adapted to exactly this: classical statistics computed across streams, plus physical-model tests — most famously Ising model simulations, whose exactly known answers make them exquisitely sensitive integration tests for correlated randomness [Mascagni & Srinivasan 2000]. The counter-based generators of Random123 were validated with both BigCrush and inter-stream variants of it for the same reason [Salmon et al. 2011].

NIST SP 800-90B: health tests for entropy sources

Everything else on this site tests output offline, after the fact. SP 800-90B addresses live hardware: an entropy source in production must run continuous health tests that catch catastrophic failure in real time. The two mandated tests are deliberately minimal — the Repetition Count Test (does the source emit the same sample over and over? a stuck ring oscillator, a dead diode) and the Adaptive Proportion Test (does one value dominate a window?). Alongside them, the standard defines the offline entropy estimation track (IID vs non-IID estimator suites) used in validation. The contrast with SP 800-22 is the point: health tests are tripwires with per-sample budgets measured in nanoseconds, not statistics aiming at power [NIST SP 800-90B].

AIS-31: the evaluation methodology

Germany's BSI takes the position that testing output is not enough: for a physical RNG to be certified under Common Criteria, the manufacturer must supply a stochastic model of the noise source — a physical argument for where the entropy comes from and how much — and then statistical procedures verify the model, not merely the output. AIS-31 defines functionality classes (PTG.1/2/3 for physical sources, DRG.x for deterministic, NTG.1 for non-physical non-deterministic) and two batteries of tests (procedures A and B: monobit, poker, runs, long-run, autocorrelation, uniform-distribution and entropy tests) keyed to those classes [Killmann & Schindler 2011] [Killmann & Schindler 2002]. A 2024 revision (version 3.0) aligns the framework with the NIST SP 800-90 series — the two regulatory worlds converging on the same shape: model the source, health-test continuously, statistically test as confirmation [BSI AIS-31 v3.0].
