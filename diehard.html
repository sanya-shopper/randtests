Diehard (Marsaglia, 1995)

The battery that made randomness testing a contact sport. George Marsaglia had spent two decades showing that the generators everyone used were defective; Diehard packaged the demonstration as software anyone could run, and the textbook generators of the era did not survive it.

History and intent

Diehard shipped in 1995 on "The Marsaglia Random Number CDROM," an NSF-supported project from Florida State University that paired the test battery with five billion pre-tested random bits [Marsaglia 1995]. The design brief was stringency. The classical tests were, by then, too easy: generators tuned to pass frequency, gap and poker tests could still carry lattice structure, bit-level correlations and rank defects. Marsaglia's tests attack exactly those weaknesses — many of them his own inventions from the preceding decades, including the birthday-spacings test [Marsaglia 1985] and the monkey tests [Marsaglia & Zaman 1993].

Input requirements

Diehard reads a fixed binary file of about 10–12 MB of 32-bit integers — roughly 90 million bits — and each test uses hard-coded sample sizes; several examine only particular bit positions of each word. The format is a fossil of its era: big enough to embarrass 1990s generators, three orders of magnitude too small to trouble a modern one [Marsaglia 1995].

Strengths, weaknesses, legacy

Strengths. Historically decisive: Diehard retired a generation of defective generators and set the adversarial tone every later suite inherited. Most of its test ideas — birthday spacings, matrix rank, sparse occupancy, minimum distance — remain in active service inside Dieharder, TestU01 and PractRand.

Weaknesses. Fixed, unadjustable sample sizes and a tiny (by modern standards) input file — the two limitations L'Ecuyer and Simard cite as motivation for TestU01 [L'Ecuyer & Simard 2007]. The user must interpret raw p-values by eye, with Marsaglia's own wry warning that "p happens": isolated extreme p-values occur even for perfect input. OPERM5's covariance matrix has a long-documented bug, and the overlapping-sums reference distribution is systematically wrong — both defects inherited and flagged by Dieharder [Brown et al.]. It was never designed for cryptographic evaluation.

Legacy. Unmaintained since Marsaglia's death in 2011, with the original FSU site surviving only in mirrors and archives. Its successors are the next three pages: Dieharder reimplemented and parameterised it, NIST STS standardised the cryptographic corner, and TestU01 out-stringencied it — completing the cycle Diehard itself began, since generators that pass Diehard fail Crush by the dozen [L'Ecuyer & Simard 2007].
